# M7.3: the case a human owns, on screen

## 0. Why this step exists

M4 built handoff, assignment, approval and closure. Seven API routes, a permission each,
and a role matrix in which a supervisor assigns and decides but does not work a case. None
of it has ever been reachable from the demo. A supervisor signs in, the page tells them
truthfully that they hold `ticket:read`, `ticket:assign` and `escalation:decide`, and then
gives them the same chat box a customer gets.

M6.9 measured what that costs without meaning to. A test conversation escalated, ticket 1
opened, and nothing in the environment could ever pick it up; the open ticket then held the
instance awake, because M6.3's idle watcher correctly counts an open ticket as work in
progress. The row had to be deleted by hand.

There is a second reason, and it is the sharper one. **The demo has no account that can
work a ticket.** `ava` and `ivan` are customers, `sam` is a supervisor, and a supervisor
deliberately does not hold `ticket:work`. So the escalation path in the deployed demo runs
one way: a case can be created and never resolved.

M7's acceptance line is "user actions, state and error display". This is the last of the
three, and the one that decides whether the demo can tell a whole story.

## 1. Change card

- **Stage and step**: M7.3
- **Type**: feature
- **The specific problem**: three milestones of case handling exist and cannot be reached,
  and no account in the demo can work a case even if they could
- **The single theme of this change**: a case a human owns is visible, assignable, workable
  and closable from the page
- **Control variables this change does not touch**: the model, the simulated user, the
  judge, the task sets, the upstream commit, the price snapshot, the agent, the guard, the
  permission matrix, the persistence, the deployment shape, and every baseline
- **Explicitly out of scope**:
  - **New rules about who may do what.** M4 decided that and the API enforces it. This step
    shows it. Section 1.3.
  - **Notifications.** A person finds a case by looking, not by being told.
  - **The audit trail as a screen.** `audit:read` exists and the route exists; rendering it
    is worth its own step and this one is already three parts.
  - **Editing a ticket's text.** A case is what happened, not a document.
- **Expected benefit**: the demo can show a refusal becoming a case, a person taking it,
  working it and closing it, which is the claim the whole project makes
- **Possible cost**: a page with sections that appear for some accounts and not others,
  which is a new way for the page to be wrong. Section 1.3 is about what that hiding is and
  is not.

### 1.1 The three parts

**An account that can work a case.** A fourth demo account, `mia`, with the `agent` role.
Terraform's `demo_accounts` gains it and the password is set out of band, as M6.7
established. Because of M6.8 this is a parameter change and a restart rather than a new
machine, which is the first time that mechanism pays for something other than a version.

**A ticket id the page can use.** `ChatResponse` gains `ticket_id`. M7.2 found that the
number exists only inside the service's prose, and had the page leave it there rather than
dig it out with a regular expression. This is the field that makes that unnecessary.

**The page.** Three sections beyond the conversation: the cases this account can see, the
one it is working, and the escalations waiting for a decision.

### 1.2 What each account sees

One page. Which sections appear comes from the permissions `/me` reports, which come from
the database.

| Section | Permission | customer | agent | supervisor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conversation | - | yes | yes | yes |
| Cases | `ticket:read` | no | theirs only | all |
| Work this case | `ticket:work` | no | yes | **no** |
| Assign | `ticket:assign` | no | no | yes |
| Approvals | `escalation:decide` | no | no | yes |

The two `no`s in the supervisor column are the point of the table. A supervisor assigns and
decides and cannot perform the work, because an approver who also performs the action is
not an approver. That rule has been in `auth.py` since M3 and enforced by the API since M4;
this is the first time anybody can see it.

### 1.3 Hiding a control is not enforcement

Every section is hidden when the permission is absent, and every route still refuses a
caller who does not hold it. Those are two different things and only the second is a
control.

The page hides sections because offering an action that will be refused is a bad interface,
not because hiding it prevents anything. `tests/test_auth.py` already asserts the refusals
route by route, and this step adds nothing to that. What it adds is a check in the other
direction: a section is visible exactly when its permission is held, so a page cannot
quietly start showing a control it has no business offering.

### 1.4 Why the agent gets their own session

Working a case opens a session derived from the ticket and the assignee, not the customer's
own conversation. M4 chose that and this step only surfaces it, but it is worth stating on
the page: the customer's conversation belongs to the customer, and M3's ownership check
enforces that against everyone including the person helping them.

The consequence a viewer should be able to see: an agent working ticket 4 is acting on the
customer's orders, under the customer's identity, in a session of their own, and the audit
trail records which subject did it.

### 1.5 What the customer sees while a person has their case

Nothing new, and that is deliberate. M7.2 already gave them the state and closed the box.
When the case is closed the session runs again, which the customer discovers by sending a
message, and that is the behaviour M4 built.

A notification would be a better product and a worse demonstration: it would add a
mechanism nobody has asked about to a step whose job is to make an existing one visible.

### 2.1 Decisions made during the work

**One new route, and it is a read.** Assignment takes a subject, and a subject is an opaque
identifier nobody can type from memory. Without a way to list candidates the control would
exist and be unusable, so `GET /assignable` answers with the accounts holding `ticket:work`.
It decides nothing new: it is gated on `ticket:assign`, the same permission as assigning,
and it reads what the database already said.

**`/me` still does not answer with a subject, and `/assignable` does.** Naming somebody
else is the one action that needs one. A page that never sees its own subject cannot put it
somewhere it does not belong.

**The ticket id is read from the database, not carried by the agent.** The API looks up the
open case for the session, and only for the two statuses that can have one. Putting it on
`TurnResult` would have meant editing a type the benchmark also builds, and M7.1's claim
about an untouched agent would have stopped being true for the sake of an integer.

**The page re-reads after every action rather than patching a row.** The service decides
what a case looks like after an assignment or a closure, and a page that guessed would show
a state the database does not have.

**A failed read does not empty the tables.** `with_cases` returns its failure instead of
raising, and the last known rows stay on screen: an empty table that means "the query
failed" reads as "there are no cases", which is worse than a stale one with an error beside
it.

### 2.2 What the demo could not do before

Not a matter of polish. `ava` and `ivan` are customers, `sam` is a supervisor, and a
supervisor deliberately holds no `ticket:work`. **No account in the demo could work a
case.** An escalation was a one-way door: the case opened, nothing could pick it up, and
M6.9 measured the cost of that without meaning to - a test escalation left ticket 1 open,
the idle watcher correctly counted it as work in progress, the instance stayed awake, and
the row had to be deleted by hand.

`mia` closes the door. Her retail identity is Ivan's, so an agent working Ava's case is
visibly acting on somebody else's orders under her own account, which is the thing the
audit trail exists to record.

## 3. Comparison

### 3.1 The thresholds from section 1.6, measured

Driven end to end against the real API with authentication on: three accounts, three roles,
tokens minted from a key pair generated for the run, a real model call for the escalation,
and the local PostgreSQL underneath. What follows is that run.

**A case can be taken all the way.**

| Step | Who | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Escalate | customer | `handed_off`, then `waiting_for_human`, both naming case 5 |
| Assign | supervisor | case 5 is `assigned` to the agent |
| Work | agent | session `t5-2cbdb70167f8`, for `ava_moore_2033` |
| A write inside it | agent | `needs_approval` |
| Decide it | supervisor | `approved` |
| Close | agent | case 5 is `resolved` |
| The next message | customer | `answered`, and no case |

The last row is the one that closes the loop: the customer's session was suspended behind
the case for the whole of it and runs again once the case is closed.

**A section is visible exactly when its permission is held**, and the service refuses the
same way whatever the page shows:

| Route | customer | agent | supervisor |
|---|---|---|---|
| `GET /tickets` | 403 `ticket:read` | 200 | 200 |
| `GET /assignable` | 403 | 403 | 200 |
| `POST /tickets/5/session` | - | 200 | **403 `ticket:work`** |
| `GET /approvals` | - | **403 `escalation:decide`** | 200 |
| `POST /approvals/1/decision` | - | **403** | 200 `approved` |

**A supervisor cannot work a case**, and cannot route one to themselves either:

```
assign to themselves               403  A supervisor cannot assign a ticket to themselves.
supervisor opens the case session  403  This account does not have ticket:work.
```

That is the rule this project has claimed since M3 - an approver who also performs the
action is not an approver - produced rather than described.

**The ticket id comes from the API.** `handed_off` and `waiting_for_human` both answered
with `ticket_id: 5`; every other status answered `null`, and no id is parsed out of a
sentence anywhere in the page.

**The agent works in their own session.** `t5-2cbdb70167f8` against the customer's
`m73-b2f2bd6e`, on the customer's retail identity. The customer's conversation is never
re-entered by anybody else.

**Adding the account replaces nothing:**

```
# aws_cognito_user.demo["mia"] will be created
# aws_ssm_parameter.identities will be updated in-place
Plan: 1 to add, 1 to change, 0 to destroy.
```

A targeted plan, because the full base layer refreshes Cloudflare records and needs a token
this session does not hold. The two resources the account change touches are the two above.

**Degradation**: with authentication off the service answers as `demo`, which holds no
ticket permission, so the page in that mode is a conversation and nothing else.

**Two thresholds were not met, and neither is a detail.**

**"Nobody decides their own escalation" cannot be produced by this demo, and the threshold
was wrong to ask for it.** Raising an escalation needs `ticket:work` and deciding one needs
`escalation:decide`; no role in `ROLE_PERMISSIONS` holds both, which is the separation of
duties working. So the refusal exists in the store, is covered by its tests, and has no
sequence of demo actions that reaches it. The threshold is retired with that reason rather
than reworded into something this step happens to satisfy. What was measured instead is the
neighbouring refusal, which is reachable: an agent who raised the request cannot read the
approval queue at all, let alone decide it.

**"The four accounts still sign in" was not verified.** It needs the apply, a password for
`mia` set out of band, and the deployed pool. This step does none of the three, so the
claim is deferred to that deployment rather than asserted here. What is verified is that
the change to `demo_accounts` adds one user and destroys nothing.

## 4. Failure analysis and conclusion

### 4.1 What this changes about the demo

The demo can now tell a whole story: a customer asks for something the policy does not
allow, the agent refuses and hands off, a supervisor sees the case and gives it to somebody
who can work it, that person acts on the customer's orders in a session of their own, a
write they attempt is held for the supervisor to decide, and closing the case gives the
customer their conversation back.

Every one of those steps was already enforced. None of them could be seen.

### 4.2 Limits of these results

- **An agent cannot discover work.** `GET /tickets` narrows to the caller's own
  assignments for anyone without `ticket:assign`, so an agent's list is empty until a
  supervisor pushes a case to them. Measured: the agent's `/tickets` answered `[]` while
  the case sat open and unassigned. That is M4's design and this step surfaces it without
  changing it, but somebody reading the page for the first time will wonder where the work
  is.
- **"Nobody may decide their own escalation" cannot be produced in this demo.** Requesting
  one needs `ticket:work` and deciding one needs `escalation:decide`, and no role holds
  both. The API enforces it and the store's tests cover it; the page cannot show it.
- **No browser again.** The flow was driven through the API with the page's own helpers
  tested beside it. What a Gradio dropdown of assignees looks like to a person is not
  measured.
- **`mia` does not exist yet in the deployed pool.** The Terraform change is in the
  repository and planned; applying it, setting the password out of band and restarting the
  instance so the seeding runs are three operator actions this step does not take.
- **The approval expiry was not exercised.** A request that expires between being raised
  and being decided answers 409, and this run decided within seconds.

### 4.3 Conclusion

**PASS on eight of the ten thresholds, with one retired and one deferred.** Both are named
in section 3.1 rather than left to be noticed.

A case was escalated, assigned, worked, escalated again for a decision, decided, and
closed, with every refusal along the way coming from the service and naming the permission
it wanted. The demo has an account that can work a case for the first time.

The retired threshold is the more interesting of the two. It asked the demo to produce
"nobody may decide their own escalation", and measuring showed that no role can hold both
halves of that conflict, so the rule cannot be violated and therefore cannot be
demonstrated. A rule that is unreachable by construction is a better outcome than one
that has to be caught at the door, and the threshold was written without noticing the
difference.

Accepted because the capability M7's acceptance line names is present and was driven end to
end, and because the two gaps are stated with what would close them.

### 4.4 What this leaves for later

- **M7 is complete**, and with it the three stages of the page: the work is visible, the
  states say what to do, and the case work is reachable.
- **A deployment carrying M7.** It is also the second image the M6.8 rollback mechanism can
  move between, which is the measurement M6.8 said it could describe but not make.
- **Three operator actions** before the deployed demo can show any of this: apply, set
  `mia`'s password, restart.
- **The audit trail as a screen.** `audit:read` and its route have existed since M3 and M4
  and are still only reachable with a command line.

parameter alone. `docs/OPERATIONS.md` carries the check and the reasoning.
