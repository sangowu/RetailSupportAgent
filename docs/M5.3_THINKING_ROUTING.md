# M5.3: spend thinking on the turns that need it

## 0. Why this step exists

M5.2 measured what thinking is worth on this task set and found the two obvious answers
both wrong. Keeping it at `high` on every turn costs 294 285 reasoning tokens per full
run, 37% of the bill and 11 seconds of p95. Turning it off saves all of that and gets six
database outcomes wrong that the incumbent gets right every time.

Neither answer is per-turn. A retail task is twelve or thirteen model calls, and most of
them are a lookup, a clarifying question or a summary of what a tool just returned. The
turns that broke without thinking were specific: constructing a write, and counting items
in a tool result. `docs/MODEL_EXPERIMENTS.md` has named per-task routing as its own M5
feature from the start, "compared against the fixed best combination and charged for the
routing's own cost and errors".

DeepSeek's documentation makes the finer grain possible: thinking is a per-request
parameter, so the unit of the decision can be the model call rather than the task.

## 1. Change card

- **Stage and step**: M5.3
- **Type**: optimization
- **The specific problem**: every turn is charged for thinking, and M5.2 showed that only
  some turns are worse without it
- **The single theme of this change**: choosing the thinking mode per model call, and
  nothing else
- **Control variables this change does not touch**: the model id, the prompt, the guard's
  rules, the business implementation, the simulated user, the judge, the task sets, the
  upstream commit, the seed, the concurrency and the price snapshot. The two thinking
  modes used are the two M5.2 already measured on their own, `high` and disabled, so the
  endpoints of the comparison are known before the routing is written.
- **Explicitly out of scope**:
  - **No new levels.** `low` was rejected in M5.2 for costing more than `high`, and `max`
    in M0.4. The router picks between thinking and no thinking.
  - **No learned router.** No classifier, no model call to decide a model call. The
    triggers are rules over what is already in the conversation, so a decision can be
    explained and tested.
  - **No prompt change**, for the reason M5.2 gave: two changes at once cannot be told
    apart.
  - **No change to the application service.** This is the benchmark subject. The service
    would follow only if the result holds.
- **Expected benefit**: most of the cost and p95 that disabling thinking wins, without the
  quality it loses
- **Possible cost**: the router's own bill. A turn that is re-run with thinking is paid
  for twice, and a router that triggers on most turns costs more than never turning
  thinking off at all.

### 1.1 Gate zero: whether mixed modes are even legal

DeepSeek requires that, when a request carries tools, **all historical
`reasoning_content` is sent back**, and returns 400 if it is not. What the documentation
does not say is what happens to a conversation whose earlier turns ran with thinking
disabled and therefore produced no `reasoning_content` at all.

The whole design depends on the answer, so it is settled first, by a probe that costs
about a cent: a tools-carrying conversation of three turns, disabled then enabled then
disabled, each request replaying the full history under the documented rule.

If mixed modes are refused, **this step stops here and says so**. The fallback is routing
per task, decided from the opening message alone, which is a much weaker signal, and it
would be a different change card.

### 1.2 The design, if gate zero passes

**The decision is made on the output, not before it.** Nothing can be known about what a
turn requires until the turn produces something. So each turn runs with thinking disabled,
and the result is inspected: if it trips a trigger, the same turn is run again with
thinking, and the second answer is the one that is kept and sent.

**The triggers come from M5.2's measured failures, not from intuition:**

| Trigger | Why it is there |
|---|---|
| The turn proposes a write tool call | 6 of the 10 tasks lost without thinking failed the database check |
| The turn's text states a count or a filtered total over a tool result | the other 4 failed on it: 11, 11 and 12 options stated where there were 10 |
| The guard refused the previous turn | a turn that has to be redone is a turn that was already hard |

**Everything the router does is recorded per turn**: which mode ran, whether a re-run
happened, which trigger fired. Without that the result cannot be explained, and the
router's own cost cannot be separated from the model's.

### 1.3 What is measured, including the router's own errors

| Metric | Definition |
|---|---|
| Primary | reward on the full 114 tasks, paired against both incumbent runs |
| Cost | normalized cost per successful task, including every re-run turn |
| Latency | p50 and p95 of the agent's model call, counting a re-run turn as the sum of both calls |
| Router cost | share of turns re-run, and the tokens spent on discarded first attempts |
| Router error, missed | tasks that fail here and pass in both incumbent runs, where no trigger fired |
| Router error, wasted | turns re-run whose answer did not change |
| Safety | unconfirmed writes, by `scripts/audit_safety.py`, must be 0 |

A re-run turn is charged twice and timed twice. Reporting only the kept call would hide
exactly the cost this design adds.

### 1.4 The decision rule, fixed before any result is seen

The incumbent's spread is now measured rather than assumed: two full runs of the frozen
configuration scored 102 and 101, differing on 11 tasks (M5.2 section 2.3).

| Condition | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Safety | 0 unconfirmed writes |
| Quality | at least 99 of 114, that is no more than 2 below the lower incumbent run, and McNemar p >= 0.05 against both |
| Saving | normalized cost per successful task at least 15% below the incumbent |
| Latency | agent call p95 no worse than the incumbent's 15.30s |
| Completeness | every task scored, no early stop |

Accepted only if all five hold. If quality holds and the saving does not, the router is
recorded as working and not worth its overhead, which is a result and not a failure to
report. The incumbent is kept on a tie.

### 1.5 What this step spends

| Step | Estimate, off peak |
|---|---|
| Gate-zero probe | about 0.01 CNY |
| Screen on 24 dev tasks | about 2 CNY |
| Full run of 114 | 7 to 11 CNY, between the two modes' measured costs |
| One repeat, only if the first lands within one task of the incumbent | 7 to 11 CNY |

Planned about 13 CNY, at most 24. Runs go off peak, which is 10:00 to 01:00 UTC on
weekdays.

- **Baseline, previous version, task set and protocol**: `baseline-v3`
  (`20260908T121800Z`, 102/114) and its measured repeat (`20260909T113217Z`, 101/114),
  with thinking disabled (`20260909T103513Z`, 97/114) as the cheap end of the range. Task
  sets, stack and protocol version are unchanged.

## 2. Gate zero, and the constraint it did not catch

### 2.1 The probe: mixed modes are legal

Three turns against the real endpoint, disabled then enabled then disabled, each request
replaying the whole history under the documented rule
(`evaluation/capability_checks/20260909T131214Z/thinking_mix.json`):

| Turn | Mode | Emitted a tool call | History carried reasoning | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | disabled | yes | no | accepted |
| 2 | enabled | no | no | accepted |
| 3 | disabled | no | yes | accepted |

Both directions are accepted, so a conversation may mix the two modes and gate zero
passes.

### 2.2 The constraint the probe missed, found by running it

The first routed screen hit `The reasoning_content in the thinking mode must be passed
back to the API` on 2 of 24 tasks, and upstream retried those tasks from the beginning,
so the failing request never reached the saved trace. Capturing it took wrapping the
model call, and the capture found nothing at first because `tau2.agent.llm_agent` binds
`generate` by name at import time: patching the module it came from left the agent
calling the original.

The captured request is unambiguous. It is a thinking-enabled call whose history contains
assistant messages with tool calls and **no `reasoning_content` on any of them**, and
whose last message is a tool result. The turn was mid-chain: the model had called a tool
with thinking off, the result had come back, and the router then asked for the
continuation with thinking on.

Put beside the probe, the rule is:

> Within one assistant turn - a chain of tool calls and results with no user message in
> between - the mode cannot change. Once a user message closes the turn, the next turn
> may start in either mode.

The probe's turn 2 was legal precisely because a user message separated it from the
tool-calling turn before it. That difference was not deliberate, and it is why running the
design was worth more than reading about it.

### 2.3 What this does to the design

The reactive router in section 1.2 decides after seeing the turn's output, which is
usually mid-chain: the model looks up the order, the result comes back, and only then does
it propose the write. That is exactly the moment the provider refuses to switch.

So the trigger cannot be reactive. The decision has to be made where a user message
closes the previous turn, from what is known at that point: the user's own message and the
state of the conversation. That is a different router with different triggers and its own
false-positive rate, and it needs its own change card rather than an edit to this one.

The work stops here, with the constraint measured and the router that cannot work
recorded. Nothing was adopted and no baseline changed.

## 3. What routing could be worth, at its best

Section 2.3 leaves one legal design: decide at the user-message boundary and hold that
mode for the whole turn. Before building it, the same raw results that exposed the
constraint can put a ceiling on what it could ever pay.

`baseline-v3`'s conversations were split at every user message, and each segment was
marked as needing thinking if it contains a write, a confirmation request, or a claim
about a quantity. This is the **oracle**: it knows after the fact what each segment turned
out to contain, which no router can.

| | |
|---|---|
| Agent model calls in the run | 1428 |
| Calls in segments containing a write or a confirmation | 485 (34%) |
| Adding segments containing a quantity claim | 516 (36%) |
| User-turn segments that would run with thinking | 325 of 1002 (32%) |

Two conclusions follow, and neither depends on how good the router is.

**The latency threshold is unreachable.** 36% of calls keep thinking, and p95 is decided by
the slowest 5%, which are all thinking calls. The decision rule in 1.4 asks for a p95 no
worse than the incumbent's 15.30s and counts a p95 improvement as one of the two ways to
earn the change. A perfect router scores nothing on that axis.

**The cost ceiling is about 24%.** Thinking is 37% of the run's cost (20.305 CNY with it,
12.807 without). Removing it from 64% of the calls saves about 24%, before any mistake.
That clears the 15% threshold in 1.4 with little room, and every miss costs a task while
every false alarm gives some of it back.

### 3.1 Why the model is not asked to route either

Letting the model under test judge each user message and pick the mode was considered and
is not built. It is one extra call per user turn, about 1000 per full run, and it makes the
same prediction from the same information, in advance of knowing what the turn will do. It
eats into the 24% while adding a failure mode that a rule does not have.

## 4. Conclusion

**Not adopted, and the reason is a measurement rather than a preference.**

- A conversation may mix thinking modes, but not inside a single assistant turn. Measured,
  with the failing request captured.
- The reactive router in this card's section 1.2 is therefore impossible, because its
  decision point is always mid-turn.
- The legal alternative, deciding at the user-message boundary, has a ceiling of about 24%
  on cost and exactly zero on p95, before any routing mistake. Against that, the risk it
  runs is the one M5.2 already priced: six of the ten tasks that thinking-off loses fail on
  the database state, not on wording.

What is kept, because it cost real money to learn and should not have to be learned twice:

| Kept | Why |
|---|---|
| `scripts/probe_thinking_mix.py` | the gate-zero probe, and the record in `evaluation/capability_checks/20260909T131214Z` |
| The router in `bench_agent.py`, behind `--route-thinking` | off by default; the benchmark subject is unchanged without the flag, which a test pins |
| 13 tests | the triggers, and that both attempts of a re-run are paid for |
| Section 3's ceiling | the arithmetic that says how much any future router can win |

### 4.1 Not verified

- **The boundary router itself.** It was not built, so its accuracy is unmeasured. The
  ceiling in section 3 is arithmetic over an oracle, not a result.
- **Whether the constraint is version-specific.** It was measured against
  `deepseek-v4-pro` on 2026-09-09. A provider may change it, and the probe is the way to
  re-check rather than assume.
- **The routed screen's numbers.** The one routed run (`20260909T131750Z`, 21/24) is not
  reported as a result: two of its tasks were retried whole after the 400, and the
  discarded attempts are not in the trace, so its cost is understated and its score is a
  survivor. It is left on disk and not compared. Two 6-task runs made while capturing the
  failing request (`20260909T133604Z`, `20260909T134021Z`) are diagnostics and are not
  results either.
- **Other providers.** DashScope's thinking switch was not tested for the same
  restriction.
